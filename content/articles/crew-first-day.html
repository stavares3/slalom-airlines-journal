The first line check

Listen to this story

Every new member of our cabin crew flies their first working trip with an examiner on board. We call it the line check, and no amount of training school prepares you for the particular feeling of the morning it happens. This is the story of mine, a Seattle to Denver turn on a Thursday in June, told with enough distance now to admit which parts I got wrong.

I arrived ninety minutes early, which the examiner later told me she noted, and not as a compliment. Early is good. Ninety minutes early means you will be tired by top of descent. I checked the emergency equipment twice, counted meal carts that were not mine to count, and greeted the captain with a handshake so formal he asked whether I was joining the airline or acquiring it.

The checklist is there so your hands know what to do while your heart figures out how to live at this altitude.

Boarding is where the job actually starts, and boarding is a river. One hundred and sixty people, each carrying a bag and a mood, and your work is to read both without appearing to read either. My examiner stood at door two and did nothing, which is the hardest thing a teacher can do. A gentleman in row 9 could not lift his bag. A mother in 22 needed the bassinet row she had not booked. A commuter in 4 wanted only to be left alone, and leaving someone alone, done attentively, is also service.

The flight itself was smooth until it was not. We hit ninety seconds of honest chop over the Rockies during the coffee run, and I learned the difference between knowing the procedure and being the procedure. Cups capped, cart braked and stowed, a hand on the seatback and a word to the nervous flyer in 17C, all of it done before I had consciously decided to do any of it. Training is strange that way. It waits inside you until the airplane asks for it.

We landed in Denver four minutes early. The examiner signed my paperwork on the jump seat with the flourish of someone who has signed a thousand of them, then told me the two things I keep taped inside my locker to this day. Slow is smooth. And the passengers are not interrupting the job, they are the job.
