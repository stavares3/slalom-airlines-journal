The electric quiet

Listen to this story

The first thing you notice is what is missing. Our electric demonstrator lifted off from Boeing Field just after nine on a gray Tuesday, and the loudest sound in row 14 was the landing gear folding into the belly. No spool. No shove of turbofan air. Just a firm push, a hum you feel in the armrest more than hear, and the city dropping away in something close to silence.

The airplane is a thirty seat proving aircraft, one of two we fly on short runs between Seattle and Portland while the engineers collect data and the rest of us collect impressions. From 14A, a window just aft of the wing, the impressions arrive fast. You hear the air itself. At two hundred knots the slipstream is one long steady breath over the skin of the wing, closer to a sailplane than to anything you have boarded with a boarding pass.

Passengers keep asking when we plan to start the engines. We are usually passing eight thousand feet when they ask.

The cabin crew have their own measurements. Conversation carries at normal volume across the aisle, all the way through climb. The service cart is suddenly the loudest thing aboard, and the galley chime, tuned for a cabin full of engine noise, now reads as urgent from the last row. The engineering team's meters put cruise at 58 decibels, which is the level of a library reading room. A regional turboprop runs twenty decibels higher, and every three of those is a doubling of sound energy. The gap is not subtle.

What surprised me is what the quiet does to time. Forty minutes to Portland feels longer in the best way. People read. People sleep, genuinely sleep, on a morning hop. You hear the flight deck acknowledge a frequency change through the door, faintly, like a radio in another room, and it feels less like machinery and more like crewed sailing.

Honesty is part of this journal, so here is the rest. Batteries are heavy, range is short, and this airplane will not cross an ocean in my career. It is a proving aircraft, and what it proves is sound. The proving runs continue through the winter schedule, the findings will be published when the program closes, and the first thing every visiting colleague says on the jet bridge afterward is the same thing you will hear in this episode: say that again, it was too quiet to believe.
