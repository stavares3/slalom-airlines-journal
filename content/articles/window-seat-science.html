Window seat science

Every seat map has a quiet hierarchy, and the people who fly for a living read it at a glance. Ask a pilot where they sit when they ride in the back and the answer comes fast: on the left, at a window, forward of the wing. Seat A is the captain's side of the airplane. This is the case for booking it, and for booking it at sunrise.

Start with the flight deck. The captain flies from the left seat, a convention nearly as old as instrument flying itself. Left turns dominate the traffic pattern, the taxi tiller sits on the left, and generations of procedures assume the view from that window. When you settle into 11A you are seeing, give or take a few rows, what the captain sees: the same runway edge on takeoff, the same slice of horizon in the turn.

Then the geometry. Fly south out of Seattle on a clear morning, toward Denver or the islands, and the sun comes up on the left side of the ship. From the A side the light arrives low and sideways, the kind photographers wait a year for. It catches the winglet first, then the ridgelines of the Cascades, then the whole quilted floor of cloud below. The F side gets a bright cabin. The A side gets the show.

The left window is the working window. I have watched a thousand sunrises from it and I am not tired of it yet.

There is also the small theater of the gate. Airliners board through their left doors, so the A side faces the terminal glass while the right side faces the ramp. Choose A and your last view before pushback is of the building you just left. Choose F and you get the honest choreography of loaders, fuelers, and marshallers. Both have their partisans. At sunrise, the argument settles itself.

A few practical notes. Book a morning departure that tracks south or southeast and the light does the rest. Sit forward of the wing for the cleanest view, row 10 to 14 on most of our fleet. Keep the shade up for takeoff, which you should be doing anyway. And if the crew ask you to lower it for the movie hour on a longer sector, a polite request to keep one notch of dawn usually lands well.

The window seat costs nothing extra on most fares, and seat A costs the same as seat F. One of them puts you on the captain's side of the sky at the exact hour the sky earns it. Science may be a strong word. Call it accumulated observation, which is most of what flying is.
